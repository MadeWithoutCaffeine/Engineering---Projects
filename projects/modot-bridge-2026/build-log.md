## 1. How I learned about the competition

The MoDOT bridge competition is offered every year to participating schools in Missouri. My school has been involved for almost as long as it has been available. I had known about it for probably three years, but this was my first year actually doing it, and I had not had the chance to prepare beforehand.

Our science teacher introduced the competition to my whole class and gave us the handouts. She currently teaches me college-level science and also runs our robotics club, including its paperwork and organization. She has been involved with the robotics club for about 16 years. From what I understand, she has participated in the bridge competition for all but about its first two years. She ordered our materials and has helped other students design and build their own bridges.

## 2. Understanding the goal

The goal is to build a bridge that holds a lot of weight compared with the weight of the bridge itself. As I understand the scoring, the amount the bridge holds is divided by the bridge's own weight. That means strength alone is not enough. Every piece adds weight, so the challenge is deciding where material actually helps and where it is unnecessary.

I understood that balance going into the project, but time complications meant I did not optimize the weight as much as I wanted to this year. My focus became getting a simpler bridge built with a strong base.

## 3. Researching shapes and exploring designs

I went through many designs before deciding what to build. I researched shapes found in nature, including triangles and hexagons, and looked at arrangements used in lightweight structures. I wanted to understand how the shape of a structure affects its strength and how I could apply that to the bridge.

I also considered paired side trusses, a Pratt-style option, and an 18-inch braced queen-post proposal before choosing the final design.

## 4. Visualizing the options and recognizing my limits

I compared drawings and several truss arrangements to understand how the parts might fit together. It was useful for exploring options, but those comparisons couldn't tell me what the finished bridge would actually hold.

The biggest limitation was that I did not have enough time or extra materials to test all the ideas. Extra test materials were not issued to us, so I would probably have needed to get those myself. I could research possible solutions, but I could not thoroughly compare joint strength, load distribution, different shapes, which pieces would snap, or how thick individual members needed to be.

I wanted to minimize weight while keeping the bridge's shape, stability, and strength. Without enough physical testing, I could not confidently work out all those details. That led me toward a simpler design.

### Drawing my own final design and preparing printable blueprints

I drew the final design on paper and added the dimensions I needed for construction. I wanted clear plans that I could print and keep in front of me while building.

I went back through the measurements and competition guidelines before deciding what to use. Checking the plans helped me think through the design, but testing the finished bridge was still the way to find out how it performed.

## 5. Preparing materials and starting construction

Before building, I spent time planning which pieces I would use and how to get the most usable sticks out of the material available. Some of the photos may show this preparation before construction actually started.

Construction took roughly four to four and a half days. Researching and exploring designs took additional time before that.

## 6. Choosing a simpler bridge and strengthening the base

I went back and forth between ideas for a while before settling on an initial design. As I started planning it out roughly, I realized that its structural concept was still untested. Without extra materials, I could not try out the additional joints or see how the different parts would work together.

I was also unfamiliar with the competition rules and how quickly I needed to finish. Given those time constraints, I decided against taking that risk this year. I chose a simpler design that I felt was more likely to pass the requirements. I put more emphasis on a strong base. The later test ended after the string bracing loosened and slipped, so it did not establish the frame's full load capacity.

I did not push the strength-to-weight balance as far as I wanted this year. I was working with limited time and without spare material for experiments, so I had to make decisions without first testing every connection or member size. Next year, I want to prepare earlier and use testing to make those decisions more carefully.

## 7. Competition test and what happened afterward

The bridge weighed **28.65 grams** and supported **13.2 pounds**. I made a mistake when tensioning two of the strings: I tightened them slightly too much. During loading, one loosened and another slipped, which disrupted the bracing and brought the test to an end.

When I received the bridge back, it was still intact. Apart from two loose strings, it showed next to no visible signs of having been tested. The wooden frame appeared to be in nearly the same condition as before.

It was disappointing to have the test end because of a mistake in the stringing. I had spent time thinking through the frame and its base, but the way I tensioned and secured the strings mattered just as much. The result gave me a specific part of my process to improve.

Before the test, I had estimated around 90% efficiency. That was an expectation, not a verified score. The result I can record is 13.2 pounds at 28.65 grams, which works out to about 209 times the bridge's own weight. The intact appearance afterward does not prove what a corrected bridge could have held.

## 8. What I am taking from this year and planning for next year

This is my first year doing the competition, and I still have another chance next year. After building and testing it, I have a better idea of how much preparation goes into a bridge like this. Researching a shape is only part of it. I also need time to test connections, compare member sizes, think about load distribution, and decide where extra material is worth the added weight.

Next year, I want to start earlier and be better prepared. I hope to get extra test materials so I can compare ideas before committing to a final bridge. I especially want to test string anchoring, slipping, and a consistent way to set tension. I want to do a better job of keeping the bridge light while maintaining its strength, instead of relying mainly on making important sections stronger.

I am also interested in scholarship opportunities connected to the competition because I am working toward attending Missouri S&T. MoDOT's current recap says Missouri S&T and SEMO awarded scholarships to top winners this year, but that does not establish my eligibility or the terms for next year. I still value the experience, effort, and learning that this project represents.
